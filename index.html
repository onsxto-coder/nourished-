            How the day adds up

            The chef's guide

            Keep the flavour. Find your balance. A few kitchen habits that make the numbers easier.

            Build the plate first

            Fill half the plate with vegetables and fruit, a quarter with wholegrains and a quarter with protein. It works for a plated dinner and for a shared table: let the rice take its quarter, the meat or fish from the ulam take another, and fill the rest with the dish's vegetables and a side of greens.

            Count what goes into the pan

            Oil, coconut milk, sauces and garnish carry more energy than most people expect. A tablespoon of cooking oil is around 120 kcal. Weigh them once in the recipe lab and you'll know where the energy in a dish really comes from.

            Keep room for sauces

            A good sauce is what makes a dish yours, so plan for it rather than cutting it. Spoon it on, don't pour it, and count a portion like any other ingredient.

            Cook once, plan twice

            Soups, stews and braises often taste better the next day. Save the recipe, add it to tonight's dinner, then copy the day forward so tomorrow's lunch is already planned.

            Read labels per 100 g

            Compare per 100 g, not per pack or per serving, and match raw to raw and cooked to cooked. Rice, pasta and meat change weight a lot as they cook.

            If you train

            Switch your targets to fitness mode. Spread your protein over three or four meals instead of saving it for dinner, and put more of your carbohydrates around training. On low-carb days, keep vegetables at half the plate so the fibre stays.

            Be kind with the numbers

            Every figure here is an estimate. Use it to notice patterns across a week, not to judge a single meal. If a number worries you, talk it through with your doctor or a dietitian.

            How nourished works

            The method behind the numbers, and what happens to your data.

            The method

            Daily energy

            Your resting energy comes from the Mifflin–St Jeor equation:

            • Male: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5
            • Female: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161

            That figure is multiplied by an activity factor to estimate the energy you use in a day: 1.2 (mostly sitting), 1.375 (lightly active), 1.55 (moderately active), 1.725 (very active) or 1.9 (extra active). A gentle loss goal takes away 10%, a gentle gain goal adds 10%, and the target never drops below your resting energy. Results are rounded to the nearest 10 kcal.

            Macros

            Your plate balance splits the energy between carbohydrates, protein and fat, then converts it to grams at 4 kcal per gram of carbohydrate or protein and 9 kcal per gram of fat.

            Fitness mode

            Fitness mode sets protein by body weight instead of as a share of energy: 2.0 g per kilogram to lose fat, 1.6 g to maintain and 1.8 g to build muscle, never more than 40% of the day's energy. Losing fat takes 15% off your maintenance energy and building muscle adds 10%. On the balanced plate, fat is 25% of energy and carbohydrates make up the rest. On the low-carb plate, carbohydrates are 25% of energy and fat makes up the rest.

            BMI

            BMI is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. nourished shows two readings. The Asian guideline used in Singapore puts 18.5–22.9 as the healthy range, 23–27.4 as moderate risk and 27.5 or above as high risk. The WHO international ranges are 18.5–24.9 healthy, 25–29.9 overweight and 30 or above obesity. BMI doesn't measure body fat or muscle, so treat it as one signal among many.

            Recipe nutrition

            Recipes from my sites use the estimated nutrition per serving printed on each recipe page. Where a recipe has none, you can enter your own estimate before adding it to your plan. The recipe lab adds up the values you enter for each ingredient and divides by the number of servings.
